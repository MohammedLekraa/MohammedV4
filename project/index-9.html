01. Project overview

What if you could control a 3D-printed robotic arm just by moving your own hand? That's exactly what I built here: a natural, low-cost interface bridging human motion and hardware.

The goal was to build a complete system from scratch. A custom glove tracks finger flexion and wrist orientation, sending data via Bluetooth across two ESP32 microcontrollers to mirror gestures in real time.

Beyond just making things move, the real challenge was tackling real-world engineering hurdles: handling analog sensor calibration, filtering IMU drift, and designing custom PCBs to keep wiring clean.

02. System Architecture

For the arm to copy my hand, we need sensors to capture motion, a wireless link to transmit data, and actuators to reproduce it. The system is divided into two nodes: the glove, which captures, and the arm, which acts.

04. Control glove design and integration

The glove is the part that captures the movement. Hover or click each label to see what every element does.

Five flex sensors, one on each finger, measure flexion and extension. They must be firmly attached yet flexible: with some common adhesives the sensor became stiff once dry and no longer bent properly.

The MPU6050 IMU sits at the wrist and records its rotation. It is powered at 5 V directly from the board, because the GY-521 module has an internal regulator that provides the 3.3 V the sensor needs.

The electronics (microcontroller, battery and PCB) are in a box on the forearm, held with velcro. It has one opening for the glove wiring and two for the power connector, so it can be plugged and unplugged without opening it. During prototyping the wiring stays visible to make debugging easy.

05. Flex sensor: finger flexion-extension

How the sensor works

The sensor contains an internal resistor whose resistance increases as it bends. When flat, its resistance is within the range of 17.5 kΩ to 32.5 kΩ. When bent to the maximum (180°), its resistance is at least double the initial value.

From resistance to number: voltage divider and ADC

The microcontroller measures voltages between 0 and 3.3 V, not resistances. So each sensor forms a voltage divider with a 47 kΩ fixed resistor (the sensor is on top and the fixed resistor at the bottom; the voltage is taken at the midpoint). When the finger bends, Rflex rises and Vout drops.

Vout = Vin · Rfix / (Rfix + Rflex)

Next, the microcontroller's 12-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) transforms that voltage into a discrete numerical value between 0 and 4095. These digital numbers are the actual data that the program reads, processes, transmits wirelessly, and works with to compute movement.

ADC = Vout / 3.3 · 4095

Which fixed resistor to choose

At first a 10 kΩ resistor was used. It worked, but the signal covered a small range: 150 to 460 ADC units, a variation of about 310 points. SparkFun recommends 47 kΩ for these sensors. With that value the reading goes from about 1600 to 2300, a variation of about 700 points. A wider range tells small changes apart better and gives smoother servo control, so 47 kΩ was chosen.

Hover over the chart to see the values.

Characterisation & angle mapping: the map() function

To understand sensor behavior, each one was characterised by fixing the finger at various angles (0°, 45°, 90°, 135° and 180°) and recording the ADC reading. With the sensor completely flat and extended, the ADC readings are around maximum values (close to 2300), while bending it to the maximum reduces them down to the minimum range (around 1600).

The recorded points line up roughly in a straight line, with no sudden jumps or saturation. Although physically the resistive material of the sensor does not have a perfectly linear response, the scatter is small enough to approximate it reliably with a straight line across its useful working range.

To translate this reading to servo movement, Arduino's native map() function is used, which linearly maps the experimentally obtained ADC range to motor degrees (from 180° to 0°).

angle = map(flex, minFlex, maxFlex, 180, 0)

06. IMU MPU6050: wrist rotation

1. How I get degrees from the gyroscope

The gyroscope does not measure the angle but the angular velocity: how fast the sensor turns. It was set to the ±250 °/s range, because the human wrist does not exceed that speed and this gives the highest resolution: 131 units per °/s. To get degrees there are four steps: read the raw value gx, subtract the bias measured at rest, divide by 131 to get °/s, and integrate the velocity over time, that is, add ·dt to the previous angle at every step.

= (gx offset) / 131    roll_gyro = roll + · dt

2. The gyroscope has a problem: drift

The angle obtained is very smooth in the short term, but it accumulates error over time. Even small inaccuracies, such as 0.1 °/s with the sensor still, add up at every iteration. In the plot the sensor was completely motionless and yet the computed angle drifts about 3.5° in roughly fifteen minutes. With a longer test the deviation would be even larger. The gyroscope alone is not enough for a stable long-term measurement.

3. I add the accelerometer and a complementary filter

The accelerometer gives an absolute angle relative to gravity, computed with the arctangent of two of its components. It has no drift, but it is noisy and affected by linear accelerations. To take the best of each sensor I applied a complementary filter: the gyroscope dominates the fast, smooth behaviour, and the accelerometer slowly corrects the accumulated error.

roll_acc = atan2(ay, az)    roll = 0.98 · roll_gyro + 0.02 · roll_acc

4. A new problem: the jump from 180° to 179°

When testing the system with the servo, a strange behaviour appeared: on reaching about 179°, the angle suddenly became 179° and the servo made an abrupt move opposite to the real rotation. It was not a measurement error: each sensor represents the angle with a different range. The gyroscope is integrated continuously and can go past 180°, while the accelerometer, using atan2, always stays between 180° and 180°. The same position can be 181° for one sensor and 179° for the other.

5. The fix: angle unwrapping before the filter

Before applying the filter, the accelerometer angle is compared with the gyroscope angle. If the difference exceeds ±180°, both represent the same orientation on different turns, and a full turn (360°) is added or subtracted to align them.

= roll_acc roll_gyro    > 180° roll_acc 360°    < 180° roll_acc + 360°

This way the angle is continuous and stable even when passing ±180°. Before sending it to the servo it is limited between 0° and 180° and scaled by a factor of 1.82 to fit the range detected by the IMU to the servo travel. When the real rotation exceeds 180° the value saturates at the maximum, and likewise below 0°. The servo never makes abrupt moves or reversals.

07. PCB design and fabrication

To organise the wiring, two custom PCBs were designed in KiCad: one for the glove (signal conditioning and voltage dividers) and another for the arm (power interface and servo connection).

08. System power supply

The electrical design uses independent power supplies for the microcontroller and actuators, preventing voltage drops and ESP32 reboots caused by servo current peaks.

During early tests, moving multiple servos simultaneously caused reboots. To solve this, all grounds (GND) remain strictly connected, as PWM signals require a common electrical reference.

Glove board

5500 mAh rechargeable Li-ion battery, acting as a 9 V source.

Arm board

Two 18650 Li-ion cells in series at 3.2 V (~6.4 V nominal).

Servomotors

Promax FAC-363B lab power supply at 6.5 V (~1.6 A peak draw).
